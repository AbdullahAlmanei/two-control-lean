# `HT_Rz_dense`: Complete Proof and Dependency Tree

This document audits the proof rooted at
[`HT_Rz_dense`](BoykinDensity.lean) in `BoykinDensity.lean`.

The theorem is:

```lean
theorem HT_Rz_dense (theta : Real) {epsilon : Real} (hepsilon : 0 < epsilon) :
    exists C : HTCircuit, hsDistance (rz theta) (HTCircuit.eval C) < epsilon
```

In mathematical language:

> Every standard one-qubit rotation `Rz(theta)` can be approximated, to any
> positive Hilbert-Schmidt error tolerance, by a finite circuit whose only
> primitive gates are `H` and `T`.

The proof uses the concrete irrational rotations constructed by Boykin et al.,
but it is not a literal formalization of every step of Boykin's published
proof. The exact relationship is explained below.

## Scope of this audit

The dependency tree was checked against Lean's elaborated declaration using
`Expr.getUsedConstants`. It includes every human-authored project definition
and theorem in the transitive closure of `HT_Rz_dense`.

At the Mathlib boundary, this document lists the named mathematical results
used by those project declarations. It does not list kernel recursors,
typeclass projection fields, generated equation theorems, or the internal
proof terms generated by tactics such as `ring`, `linarith`, `norm_num`,
`field_simp`, `positivity`, `omega`, `module`, and `fin_cases`. Those are proof
automation mechanisms rather than additional mathematical claims.

Generated declarations with names such as `_proof_1`, `.match_1`, `.eq_1`,
and `_simp_1_3` are likewise folded into the definition or theorem that
generated them.

## One-screen mathematical proof

Write

```text
R(n, phi) = exp(i phi (n dot sigma)).
```

Boykin supplies two concrete `{H,T}` matrices `A` and `B`, an angle `lambda`,
and orthogonal unit axes `n1` and `n2`, such that

```text
A = R(n1, lambda pi),
B = R(n2, lambda pi),
lambda is irrational.
```

These are exact matrix identities. The circuits `A` and `B` are fixed; they
do not accept a continuously varying angle as input.

For every integer `j`, the rotation law gives the exact identity

```text
A^j = R(n1, j lambda pi).
```

Because `lambda` is irrational, the discrete set of angles

```text
{ j lambda pi mod 2 pi | j is an integer }
```

is dense in the circle. Thus integer powers of `A` come arbitrarily close to
every rotation about `n1`, and integer powers of `B` do the same about `n2`.
In particular, for arbitrary real `alpha`, `beta`, and `gamma`, and any
positive local error tolerance, one can choose integers `j`, `k`, and `l`
with

```text
A^j approximately R(n1, alpha),
B^k approximately R(n2, beta),
A^l approximately R(n1, gamma).
```

The standard `z`-axis is expressed in the orthonormal frame

```text
n1, n2, n3 = n1 cross n2.
```

An explicit Euler-angle inversion then produces `alpha`, `beta`, and `gamma`
such that

```text
R(z, phi) = R(n1, alpha) R(n2, beta) R(n1, gamma).
```

This Euler equation is exact. It solves the axis problem: the powers of `A`
and `B` approximate angles only about `n1` and `n2`, whereas the target is a
rotation about `z`.

The project's convention for `Rz` satisfies

```text
rz(theta) = R(z, -theta / 2).
```

Split the error budget into three pieces. The product-distance inequality
gives

```text
d(R(n1, alpha) R(n2, beta) R(n1, gamma), A^j B^k A^l)
  <= d(R(n1, alpha), A^j)
   + d(R(n2, beta), B^k)
   + d(R(n1, gamma), A^l)
  < epsilon / 3 + epsilon / 3 + epsilon / 3
  = epsilon.
```

Finally, the density theorem has supplied integers and therefore the abstract
matrices `A^j`, `B^k`, and `A^l`. Separate circuit lemmas realize every
positive and negative power by a finite `{H,T}` word. Therefore
`A^j B^k A^l` is the evaluation of an actual `HTCircuit`.

The whole proof can be summarized by the chain

```text
rz(theta)
  = R(z, -theta/2)
  = R(n1, alpha) R(n2, beta) R(n1, gamma)
  approximately A^j B^k A^l
  = eval(CA(j) ++ CB(k) ++ CA(l)).
```

## Why this proof works

The preceding summary describes the route, but it does not yet explain why
these particular ingredients are necessary. The central difficulty is that a
finite gate set gives only discrete operations, while the target family

```text
{ rz(theta) | theta is real }
```

contains a continuously varying real parameter. The proof therefore needs a
discrete family of circuits that intersects every neighborhood of every
target. In other words, it needs density. It does **not** produce a literal
continuously tunable H/T gate. The set of finite H/T circuits remains
countable, just as the rational numbers remain countable even though they are
dense in the real numbers.

Boykin's mechanism has two parts:

1. An irrational rotation angle makes the discrete integer powers of one
   fixed gate dense in the rotations about one fixed axis.
2. Rotations about two orthogonal axes can be composed, through Euler
   decomposition, to obtain rotations about other axes, including the
   standard `z` axis.

Irrationality supplies arbitrarily accurate **angle approximations** around
each available axis. Euler decomposition rewrites the desired **axis** using
those two available axes. The product-distance inequality then converts three
separate approximations into the requested approximation of `rz(theta)`.

### The initial obstruction: `{H,T}` is discrete but `Rz` is continuous

The target theorem quantifies over every real `theta` and every positive
`epsilon`. It does not suffice to realize a large finite collection of
rotations. For each target angle, the proof must produce some finite circuit
whose matrix lies within the requested neighborhood of that target.

The gate `T` already points in the correct geometric direction: up to global
phase, it is a fixed rotation about the standard `z` axis. But its angle is a
rational fraction of a full turn. Concretely,

```text
T^8 = I.
```

Thus the sequence

```text
I, T, T^2, T^3, ...
```

is periodic. It contains only finitely many matrices and cannot approximate
an arbitrary `rz(theta)`. Having the correct axis is not enough; the available
angle must also avoid periodicity.

### Why irrationality matters

Boykin constructs an exactly implementable `{H,T}` circuit `A` satisfying

```text
A = R(n1, lambda*pi).
```

The rotation group law gives

```text
A^k = R(n1, k*lambda*pi).
```

Suppose first that `lambda` were rational, say `lambda = p/q`. Then the angles

```text
0, lambda*pi, 2*lambda*pi, 3*lambda*pi, ...
```

would repeat modulo `2*pi`. Powers of `A` would generate only finitely many
rotations about `n1`. There would be a positive gap between many desired
angles and every available power.

When `lambda` is irrational, the situation changes completely. The set

```text
{ k*lambda*pi mod 2*pi | k is an integer }
```

is dense in the circle. Therefore, for every desired angle `alpha` and every
positive angular tolerance, there is an integer `k` such that

```text
k*lambda*pi approximately alpha mod 2*pi.
```

Since axis rotations are periodic modulo `2*pi` and continuous in their
angle, this implies

```text
A^k = R(n1,k*lambda*pi) approximately R(n1,alpha).
```

The same argument applies to Boykin's second circuit

```text
B = R(n2,lambda*pi).
```

Consequently, the integer powers of the two fixed circuits form two discrete
but dense families:

```text
A^j can approximate R(n1,alpha) for arbitrary alpha;
B^k can approximate R(n2,beta) for arbitrary beta.
```

This is why the proof spends substantial effort establishing
`boykinLambda_irrational`. The irrationality theorem is not an isolated
number-theoretic curiosity. It is exactly what prevents the powers of `A` and
`B` from closing into finite cycles, and it ensures that, for any requested
angle and tolerance, some finite integer power approximates that angle.

### What irrationality does not provide

Irrationality changes the available angles, but it does not change an axis.
Every power of `A` is still a rotation about `n1`:

```text
A^j = R(n1,j*lambda*pi).
```

No matter how accurately one chooses `j`, this remains in the one-parameter
subgroup

```text
{ R(n1,alpha) | alpha is real }.
```

That subgroup is only a circle inside the three-dimensional group `SU(2)`.
Unless `n1` happens to be the standard `z` axis, density within that circle
does not directly approximate a nontrivial rotation around `z`.

This explains why a second axis is required. The role of `B` is not merely to
provide another way to approximate angles. It supplies motion transverse to
the subgroup generated by `A`.

### Why not carry out the argument using only the `Rz` axis?

There are two possible meanings of a direct `Rz`-axis argument.

#### Direct powers of `T`

This fails because `T` has a rational angle. As noted above, `T^8=I`, so its
powers are finite rather than dense.

#### Find another exactly implementable irrational `z` rotation

If one had an `{H,T}` circuit `G` with

```text
G = R(z,mu*pi)
```

for irrational `mu`, then the whole two-axis Euler layer would indeed be
unnecessary. Powers of `G` would directly approximate every standard
`z`-axis rotation.

Boykin's concrete construction does not provide such a `G`. It provides
irrational rotations around the oblique axes `n1` and `n2`. The proof must
therefore transfer the angle freedom available on those axes to the desired
`z` axis. Euler decomposition is exactly that transfer mechanism.

The resources can be summarized as follows:

| Available operation | Advantage | Limitation |
|---|---|---|
| Powers of `T` | Correct `z` axis | Rational angle, hence only finitely many powers |
| Powers of `A` | Discrete powers densely approximate angles | Only around `n1` |
| Powers of `B` | Discrete powers densely approximate angles | Only around `n2` |
| Euler products of `A`- and `B`-axis rotations | Arbitrary target axis and angle | Requires three approximate factors and error accounting |

### Why two orthogonal axes are enough

Composing rotations around different axes changes the effective axis. In
general,

```text
R(n1,alpha) R(n2,beta)
```

is not a rotation about `n1` or `n2`. The noncommutativity of the two rotation
families is precisely what allows them to explore a larger part of `SU(2)`.

For two orthogonal unit axes, an Euler theorem gives

```text
R(n,phi)
  = R(n1,alpha) R(n2,beta) R(n1,gamma)
```

for suitable real angles `alpha`, `beta`, and `gamma`.

Boykin states this for arbitrary elements of `SU(2)`. The Lean proof needs
only the special target axis `n=z`, so it proves the narrower statement

```text
R(z,phi)
  = R(n1,alpha) R(n2,beta) R(n1,gamma).
```

This is enough because Lemma 12 asks only for `Rz` approximation. The proof
does not need to establish a general synthesis theorem for every one-qubit
unitary at this point.

### Why the Euler decomposition is an exact step

The Euler theorem is not itself an approximation. It rewrites the target
exactly:

```text
R(z,phi)
  = R(n1,alpha) R(n2,beta) R(n1,gamma).
```

That exact equality is valuable because it isolates all approximation error
in the replacement of the three factors by powers of `A` and `B`. There is no
additional Euler-decomposition error to track.

The target `rz(theta)` is also related to the standard axis rotation by exact
equality under the project's sign convention:

```text
rz(theta) = R(z,-theta/2).
```

Hence the complete target conversion is exact:

```text
rz(theta)
  = R(z,-theta/2)
  = R(n1,alpha) R(n2,beta) R(n1,gamma).
```

Only after this exact conversion does approximation enter the argument.

### Motivation for each major proof chunk

The proof chunks can be read as a sequence of obstacles and solutions.

#### 1. Pauli and axis-rotation algebra

Goal:

```text
R(n,phi) = cos(phi) I + i sin(phi) (n dot sigma).
```

Why it is needed:

- Matrix exponentials are otherwise too opaque to compare directly.
- The formula lets us identify `A` and `B` as rotations.
- It exposes the scalar part `cos(phi)` and vector part `sin(phi)n`.
- It makes periodicity, unitarity, and Euler-product calculations concrete.

This layer translates analytic matrix exponentials into elementary
trigonometric and vector algebra.

#### 2. Concrete circuits for `A` and `B`

Goal:

```text
eval(boykinA_circuit) = A,
eval(boykinB_circuit) = B.
```

Why it is needed:

The density of abstract matrices does not by itself prove the existence of a
finite `{H,T}` circuit. The final witness must be an actual list of `H` and
`T` gates. These theorems connect Boykin's matrix formulas to the circuit
language required by Lemma 12.

#### 3. Geometric identification of `A` and `B`

Goal:

```text
A = R(n1,lambda*pi),
B = R(n2,lambda*pi),
||n1|| = ||n2|| = 1,
inner(n1,n2) = 0.
```

Why it is needed:

This reveals exactly what repeated circuit execution does. It also supplies
the orthogonal-axis hypothesis required by the Euler decomposition.

#### 4. Irrationality of `lambda`

Goal:

```text
lambda is irrational.
```

Why it is needed:

This makes the discrete integer powers dense among rotations around the
corresponding axis. It does not create a continuously parameterized circuit.
Without irrationality, the powers could cycle through only finitely many
matrices.

#### 5. Topological density and continuity

Goal:

```text
for every alpha and delta>0,
  some integer power A^j is within delta of R(n1,alpha),
```

and similarly for `B`.

Why it is needed:

Number theory initially gives closeness of real angles modulo `2*pi`. The
theorem we need concerns closeness of matrices measured by `hsDistance`.
Periodicity removes full turns, and continuity converts a sufficiently small
angular difference into a sufficiently small matrix distance.

#### 6. Euler decomposition of the `z` rotation

Goal:

```text
R(z,phi) = R(n1,alpha) R(n2,beta) R(n1,gamma).
```

Why it is needed:

The dense powers can approximate arbitrary angles only around `n1` and `n2`.
Euler decomposition exactly rewrites the desired `z`-axis operation as three
rotations belonging to those available axis families.

#### 7. Integer-power circuit realization

Goal:

```text
eval(CA(j)) = A^j,
eval(CB(k)) = B^k
```

for all integers, including negative ones.

Why it is needed:

The dense additive subgroup theorem returns integer coefficients. Negative
powers must therefore still be represented by finite legal circuits. This is
possible because `H^(-1)=H` and `T^(-1)=T^7`.

#### 8. Product error accumulation

Goal:

```text
d(U1 U2 U3,V1 V2 V3)
  <= d(U1,V1) + d(U2,V2) + d(U3,V3).
```

Why it is needed:

The target is factored into three rotations, each approximated independently.
This inequality guarantees that local approximation errors do not combine in
an uncontrolled way.

### How the positive `epsilon` produces a circuit

Fix arbitrary `theta` and `epsilon>0`.

#### Step 1: Rewrite the target exactly

Set

```text
phi = -theta/2.
```

Then

```text
rz(theta) = R(z,phi).
```

#### Step 2: Euler-decompose the target exactly

Choose `alpha`, `beta`, and `gamma` such that

```text
R(z,phi)
  = R(n1,alpha) R(n2,beta) R(n1,gamma).
```

#### Step 3: Divide the allowed error

Since `epsilon>0`, also `epsilon/3>0`. Apply the two density theorems to
choose integers `j`, `k`, and `l` with

```text
d(R(n1,alpha), A^j) < epsilon/3,
d(R(n2,beta),  B^k) < epsilon/3,
d(R(n1,gamma), A^l) < epsilon/3.
```

#### Step 4: Build the actual circuit

Let

```text
C = CA(j) ++ CB(k) ++ CA(l),
```

where the named power circuits satisfy

```text
eval(CA(j)) = A^j,
eval(CB(k)) = B^k,
eval(CA(l)) = A^l.
```

Circuit concatenation therefore gives

```text
eval(C) = A^j B^k A^l.
```

#### Step 5: Add the three errors

Using the exact target equalities and the three-factor product bound,

```text
d(rz(theta),eval(C))
  = d(R(n1,alpha) R(n2,beta) R(n1,gamma), A^j B^k A^l)
  <= d(R(n1,alpha),A^j)
   + d(R(n2,beta),B^k)
   + d(R(n1,gamma),A^l)
  < epsilon/3 + epsilon/3 + epsilon/3
  = epsilon.
```

This is exactly the conclusion of `HT_Rz_dense`.

### The conceptual conversion chain

The proof can be remembered as four logically separate conversions:

```text
a fixed rotation through an irrational angle
  -> integer powers dense among rotations about its fixed axis

two orthogonal axes with dense power families
  -> an exact Euler factorization of the desired z rotation

integers j, k, l and matrices A^j B^k A^l
  -> finite H/T words evaluating exactly to those powers

three local epsilon/3 bounds
  -> one global epsilon bound.
```

Or, more compactly:

```text
irrationality makes the discrete power angles dense;
orthogonal axes plus Euler exactly express the target direction;
circuit-power lemmas supply legal finite words;
the distance inequality supplies the final error guarantee.
```

## 2. Closed form and unitarity of axis rotations

### `pauliVec_sq_eq_one`

Statement:

```text
||n|| = 1  implies  (n dot sigma)^2 = I.
```

The proof does not invoke the separately proved Pauli square and
anticommutation lemmas. It expands all four matrix entries directly.

Direct leaves:

- `EuclideanSpace.norm_sq_eq`
- `Fin.sum_univ_three`
- `Matrix.mul_apply`
- `Fin.sum_univ_two`
- `Complex.I_sq`
- matrix extensionality and finite-index case splitting
- polynomial normalization by `ring`

Consequently, `pauliX_sq`, `pauliY_sq`, `pauliZ_sq`, and the three named
Pauli anticommutation theorems are not transitive dependencies of
`HT_Rz_dense`.

### `expSeries_even_sq_eq_one`

For `A^2 = I`, computes the even terms of the exponential series:

```text
((i phi A)^(2n))/(2n)! = ((-1)^n phi^(2n)/(2n)!) I.
```

Direct leaves:

- `NormedSpace.expSeries_apply_eq`
- `pow_mul`, `pow_two`, `pow_succ`
- scalar-multiplication power laws
- `Complex.I_sq`
- ring normalization

### `expSeries_odd_sq_eq_one`

Computes the odd terms:

```text
((i phi A)^(2n+1))/(2n+1)!
  = i ((-1)^n phi^(2n+1)/(2n+1)!) A.
```

## 4. Irrationality of Boykin's angle

This is the part most visibly inspired by Boykin's appendix, but the Lean
proof deliberately avoids proving Boykin's full minimal-polynomial theorem.

### `rational_angle_is_rootOfUnity`

Statement:

```text
c is rational
  -> exists n > 0, exp(i*2*pi*c)^n = 1.
```

The proof writes `c` as a rational `q` and takes `n = q.den`.

Principal Mathlib leaf:

- `Complex.isPrimitiveRoot_exp_rat q`, specifically its `pow_eq_one` field.

### `cyclotomic_rational_theorem`

This is only a compatibility name. Its proof is exactly
`rational_angle_is_rootOfUnity`.

It does not prove the paper's full equivalence between rational angles and
cyclotomic minimal polynomials.

### `boykinZeta` and `boykinPolynomial`

```text
zeta = exp(i*2*pi*lambda)
P(X) = X^4 + X^3 + (1/4)X^2 + X + 1.
```

### `boykinLambda_cos`

This duplicates the defining cosine identity in a syntactic form convenient
for the irrationality branch. It uses `Real.cos_arccos` and the same bounds as
`boykinLambda_cos_axis`.

### `exp_I_trace`

Proves the standard identity

```text
exp(i theta) + exp(i theta)^(-1) = 2 cos(theta).
```

Leaves:

- `Complex.exp_neg`
- `Complex.exp_mul_I`
- `Complex.ofReal_cos`

### `boykinZeta_trace`

Uses the double-angle identity and `boykinLambda_cos` to prove

```text
zeta + zeta^(-1) = sqrt(2) - 1/2.
```

Leaves:

- `Real.cos_two_mul`
- `Real.sq_sqrt`
- `exp_I_trace`

### `boykinZeta_satisfies_polynomial`

Combines the trace equation with

```text
(sqrt(2)-1/2)^2 + (sqrt(2)-1/2) = 7/4
```

and clears the nonzero power of `zeta` to obtain

```text
P(zeta) = 0.
```

Principal leaves:

- nonzeroness of `Complex.exp`
- `Polynomial.aeval`
- `Algebra.smul_def`
- field and ring normalization

### `boykinPolynomial_trace_eq`

For any nonzero `z` satisfying `P(z)=0`, division by `z^2` yields

```text
(z + z^(-1))^2 + (z + z^(-1)) = 7/4.
```

This is the algebraic heart of the root-of-unity contradiction.

### `not_isIntegral_seven_div_four_complex`

Proves that `7/4` is not an algebraic integer over `Z`.

The route is:

```text
7/4 integral in C
  -> 7/4 integral in Q
  -> 7/4 is the image of an integer z
  -> 4z = 7 in Z
  -> contradiction.
```

Principal Mathlib leaves:

- `isIntegral_algebraMap_iff`
- `FaithfulSMul.algebraMap_injective`
- `IsIntegrallyClosed.algebraMap_eq_of_integral`
- exact casts between `Z`, `Q`, and `C`
- integer arithmetic via `omega`

### `boykin_zeta_not_rootOfUnity`

Assume `zeta^n = 1`, with `n > 0`.

Then:

```text
zeta is integral                    by IsIntegral.of_pow
zeta^(-1) = zeta^(n-1)             from zeta^n = 1
x = zeta + zeta^(-1) is integral   by closure under addition and powers
x^2 + x = 7/4                      by boykinPolynomial_trace_eq
7/4 is integral                    by closure under addition and powers
```

This contradicts `not_isIntegral_seven_div_four_complex`.

Principal Mathlib leaves:

- `IsIntegral.of_pow`
- `isIntegral_one`
- `IsIntegral.add`
- `IsIntegral.pow`
- `inv_eq_of_mul_eq_one_right`
- natural-number subtraction arithmetic

### `boykinLambda_irrational`

Assume `lambda` is rational. Then
`cyclotomic_rational_theorem` makes `exp(i*2*pi*lambda)` a root of unity,
contradicting `boykin_zeta_not_rootOfUnity`.

Thus:

```text
Irrational lambda.
```

## 6. Density of powers of an irrational axis rotation

### Rotation group laws

`axisRotation_zero` proves `R(n,0)=I` using `exp(0)=I`.

`axisRotation_add` proves

```text
R(n,phi+psi) = R(n,phi) R(n,psi).
```

Principal Mathlib leaf:

- `Matrix.exp_add_of_commute`

The two exponents commute because both are scalar multiples of the same
matrix `pauliVec(n)`.

`axisRotation_inv` derives

```text
R(n,phi)^(-1) = R(n,-phi)
```

from `axisRotation_add` and `axisRotation_zero`.

`axisRotation_nat_pow` proves by induction:

```text
R(n,phi)^m = R(n,m*phi).
```

`zpowMatrix_axisRotation` performs the positive/negative integer case split
and proves:

```text
zpowMatrix(R(n,phi),k) = R(n,k*phi).
```

### Periodicity

`axisRotation_int_mul_two_pi` uses the closed form and the periodicity of
sine and cosine to prove:

```text
R(n,2*pi*k) = I.
```

Leaves:

- `Real.cos_int_mul_two_pi`
- `Real.sin_add_int_mul_two_pi`

`axisRotation_add_int_mul_two_pi` then proves:

```text
R(n,phi + 2*pi*k) = R(n,phi).
```

### Continuity

`continuous_axisRotation_of_unit` rewrites the rotation using
`axisRotation_closed_form` and applies continuity of:

- `Real.cos`
- `Real.sin`
- `Complex.ofReal`
- scalar multiplication and matrix addition

`continuous_hsDistance_axisRotation` unfolds `hsDistance` and composes:

- continuity of `axisRotation`,
- matrix multiplication by a constant matrix,
- finite trace summation,
- complex norm,
- squaring, division by `4`, subtraction, and square root.

### `axisRotation_powers_dense`

Inputs:

```text
U = R(n,theta),
theta/(2*pi) is irrational,
epsilon > 0.
```

Output:

```text
exists k : Z, d(R(n,alpha), zpowMatrix(U,k)) < epsilon.
```

The proof has these exact logical steps:

1. `dense_addSubgroupClosure_pair_iff.mpr` turns irrationality of
   `theta/(2*pi)` into density of the additive subgroup generated by
   `theta` and `2*pi`.
2. Continuity of the distance function at `alpha` supplies a positive input
   tolerance `delta` for output tolerance `epsilon` via
   `Metric.continuousAt_iff`.
3. `Dense.exists_dist_lt` chooses a subgroup point `x` within `delta` of
   `alpha`.
4. `AddSubgroup.mem_closure_pair` writes
   `x = k*theta + l*(2*pi)` for integers `k,l`.
5. `axisRotation_add_int_mul_two_pi` removes the `l*(2*pi)` term.
6. `zpowMatrix_axisRotation` identifies the remaining rotation with `U^k`.
7. `hsDistance_self` supplies distance zero at the target point.
8. Continuity transfers closeness of angles to Hilbert-Schmidt closeness.

This is a rigorous topological version of Boykin's sentence that irrational
`lambda` lets powers approximate any phase modulo `2*pi`.

### `boykinA_powers_dense_axis₁`

The theorem first proves

```text
(lambda*pi)/(2*pi) = lambda/2.
```

Then it uses:

- `boykinLambda_irrational.div_ratCast`
- nonzeroness of the rational `2`
- `boykinA_is_axisRotation`
- `boykin_axes_unit.1`
- `axisRotation_powers_dense`

to obtain density of integer powers of `A` around axis `n1`.

### `boykinB_powers_dense_axis₂`

The same argument uses:

- `boykinB_is_axisRotation`
- `boykin_axes_unit.2`
- the same irrationality ratio
- `axisRotation_powers_dense`

to obtain density of integer powers of `B` around axis `n2`.

## 7. Quaternion/SU(2) multiplication and the Euler formula

### Explicit cross product

The file defines `cross` coordinatewise rather than using Mathlib's

```text
cross(u,v)
  = (u1 v2 - u2 v1,
     u2 v0 - u0 v2,

It expands coordinates and uses the unit-norm and orthogonality equations.

### `su2Pair_mul`

Statement:

su2Pair(a,u) su2Pair(b,v)
  = su2Pair(a*b - inner(u,v), a*v + b*u - cross(u,v)).
```

The minus sign reflects the convention `axisRotation = exp(+i phi n.sigma)`
and the chosen order of the cross product in the explicit matrix formula.

## 8. Explicit Euler inversion for the standard z-axis

This is where the formal proof most clearly departs from the paper's broad
claim.

### `standardZAxis`

Defines `z = (0,0,1)` in `EuclideanSpace Real (Fin 3)`.

### `standardZAxis_unit`

Expands `EuclideanSpace.norm_sq_eq` and proves `||z||=1`.

### `cross_orthogonal_left` and `cross_orthogonal_right`

These are aliases of:

- `euler_cross_orthogonal_left`
- `euler_cross_orthogonal_right`

with names suited to the frame construction.

### `cross_orthogonal_unit_is_unit`

Proves Lagrange's identity in the needed special case:

```text
||v||=1, ||w||=1, inner(v,w)=0
  -> ||v cross w||=1.
```

It expands all coordinates and proves

```text
||v cross w||^2
  = ||v||^2 ||w||^2 - inner(v,w)^2
  = 1.
```

The final passage from squared norm `1` to norm `1` uses nonnegativity of
norms.

### Orthonormal frame construction

In `standardZ_axisRotation_boykin_euler`, define

```text
n3 = n1 cross n2.
```

The proof establishes:

```text
||n1|| = ||n2|| = ||n3|| = 1,
inner(n1,n2) = inner(n1,n3) = inner(n2,n3) = 0.
```

It then defines the indexed frame

```text
frame(0)=n1, frame(1)=n2, frame(2)=n3.
```

Principal Mathlib dependencies:

- `Orthonormal.linearIndependent`
- `finrank_euclideanSpace_fin`
- `LinearIndependent.span_eq_top_of_card_eq_finrank'`
- `OrthonormalBasis.mk`
- `OrthonormalBasis.coe_mk`

This turns the frame into an `OrthonormalBasis`.

### Coordinates of the standard z-axis

Define:

```text
c1 = inner(z,n1)
c2 = inner(z,n2)
c3 = inner(z,n3).
```

`OrthonormalBasis.sum_repr'` gives the exact decomposition

```text
z = c1 n1 + c2 n2 + c3 n3.
```

`OrthonormalBasis.sum_inner_mul_inner` gives Parseval's identity

```text
c1^2 + c2^2 + c3^2 = 1.
```

### Construction of Euler angles

The proof introduces complex numbers encoding pairs of real coefficients:

```text
qz = c2 + i c3
r  = ||qz||
q  = arg(qz)

pz = cos(phi) + i c1 sin(phi)
m  = ||pz||
p  = arg(pz)

betaz = m + i r sin(phi)
beta  = arg(betaz)

alpha = (p-q)/2
gamma = (p+q)/2.
```

The proof establishes:

```text
r^2 = c2^2 + c3^2
m^2 = cos(phi)^2 + (c1 sin(phi))^2
||betaz||^2 = 1
||betaz|| = 1.
```

Principal Mathlib leaves:

- `Complex.norm_add_mul_I`
- `Real.sq_sqrt`
- `Real.sin_sq_add_cos_sq`
- nonnegativity of norms

The polar-coordinate theorems

- `Complex.norm_mul_cos_arg`
- `Complex.norm_mul_sin_arg`

then give:

```text
r cos(q) = c2
r sin(q) = c3
m cos(p) = cos(phi)
m sin(p) = c1 sin(phi)
cos(beta) = m
sin(beta) = r sin(phi).
```

Elementary arithmetic gives:

```text
gamma + alpha = p
gamma - alpha = q.
```

Therefore:

```text
cos(phi) = cos(beta) cos(gamma+alpha)
cos(beta) sin(gamma+alpha) = c1 sin(phi)
sin(beta) cos(gamma-alpha) = c2 sin(phi)
sin(beta) sin(gamma-alpha) = c3 sin(phi).
```

Finally:

1. `axisRotation_eq_su2Pair` expands the target rotation.
2. `boykin_euler_product_expansion` expands the three-factor product.
3. The scalar identities match the scalar parts.
4. The coordinate identities and the exact frame decomposition match the
   vector parts.

This proves:

```text
exists alpha beta gamma,
  R(z,phi) = R(n1,alpha) R(n2,beta) R(n1,gamma).
```

The construction is total even when `qz` or `pz` is zero because Mathlib's
`Complex.arg` is a total function and the identities
`norm(z) cos(arg z) = re(z)` and `norm(z) sin(arg z) = im(z)` remain valid at
zero.

## 10. Hilbert-Schmidt product error

### `hsDistance`

For `N x N` matrices:

```text
d(U,V) = sqrt(1 - ||trace(U^dagger V)||^2 / N^2).
```

For this proof, `N=2`.

### `hsDistance_self`

For unitary `U`, proves `d(U,U)=0` using:

- `Matrix.mem_unitaryGroup_iff'`
- `Matrix.trace_one`
- `Complex.norm_natCast`
- positivity of `N`

This is used inside `axisRotation_powers_dense` when continuity is centered
at the target rotation.

### `trace_inequality`

The project proves the Wang-Zhang-style trace inequality rather than assuming
it as an axiom.

Its local dependency chain is:

```text
trace_inequality
|
|-- vecHS
|   Matrix vectorization into a PiLp Hilbert space.
|
|-- vecHS_inner_eq_trace
|   inner(vecHS(A),vecHS(B)) = trace(A^dagger B).
|
|-- normalizedVec
|   Scales vecHS by 1/sqrt(N).
|
|-- inv_sqrt_nat_sq_eq_inv
|   The normalization scalar has squared norm 1/N.
|
|-- normalizedVec_inner_eq
|   inner(normalizedVec(A),normalizedVec(B))
|     = (1/N) trace(A^dagger B).
|
|-- norm_eq_one_of_inner_self_eq_one
|-- normalizedVec_norm_one_of_unitary
|   Unitary matrices become unit vectors after normalization.
|
`-- unit_vector_trace_inequality
    The triangle inequality for the projective angle of three unit vectors.
```

`unit_vector_trace_inequality` itself uses:

- `conj_mul_eq_one_of_norm_eq_one`
- `phase_align_mul`
- `inner_self_eq_one_of_norm_eq_one`
- `sq_le_one_of_nonneg_le_one`
- Cauchy-Schwarz via `norm_inner_le_norm`
- `norm_sub_sq`
- triangle inequalities for norms
- square-root monotonicity encoded through squared inequalities
- `Complex.arg` and `Complex.norm_mul_exp_arg_mul_I` to align phases

The auxiliary `one_sub_sq_div_nat_eq` translates between division by `N` and
multiplication by `N^(-2)`.

### `hsDistance_mul_le`

For unitary factors:

```text
d(U1 U2, V1 V2) <= d(U1,V1) + d(U2,V2).
```

It forms

```text
A = U1^dagger V1
B = V2 U2^dagger
```

and uses cyclicity of trace to rewrite the product distance into the form
handled by `trace_inequality`.

Principal leaves:

- `conjTranspose_mem_unitaryGroup`
- closure of the unitary group under multiplication
- `Matrix.conjTranspose_mul`
- `Matrix.trace_mul_cycle`
- `Matrix.trace_mul_comm`
- `trace_inequality`

### `hsDistance_triple_mul_le`

Applies `hsDistance_mul_le` twice:

```text
d(ABC,A'B'C')
  <= d(AB,A'B') + d(C,C')
  <= d(A,A') + d(B,B') + d(C,C').
```

It also uses closure of the unitary group under multiplication.

## 11. Approximation of a Boykin Euler product

### `boykin_HT_approx_euler_product`

Input:

```text
alpha beta gamma : Real
epsilon > 0.
```

Output:

```text
exists C : HTCircuit,
  d(R(n1,alpha) R(n2,beta) R(n1,gamma), eval(C)) < epsilon.
```

Exact proof flow:

1. Establish `epsilon/3 > 0`.
2. Apply `boykinA_powers_dense_axis₁ alpha` to obtain integer `n1` and
   error `< epsilon/3`.
3. Apply `boykinB_powers_dense_axis₂ beta` to obtain integer `n2` and
   error `< epsilon/3`.
4. Apply `boykinA_powers_dense_axis₁ gamma` to obtain integer `n3` and
   error `< epsilon/3`.
5. Define

   ```text
   C = CA(n1) ++ CB(n2) ++ CA(n3).
   ```

6. Use `HTCircuit_eval_append`, `eval_boykinA_power_circuit`, and
   `eval_boykinB_power_circuit` to prove

   ```text
   eval(C) = zpowMatrix(A,n1) zpowMatrix(B,n2) zpowMatrix(A,n3).
   ```

7. Prove every target and approximating factor unitary:

   - target factors use `axisRotation_mem_unitaryGroup`;
   - powers of `A` and `B` use their axis-rotation identities,
     `axisRotation_inv`, and `Submonoid.pow_mem`.

8. Apply `hsDistance_triple_mul_le`.
9. Substitute the three strict `< epsilon/3` bounds.
10. Finish with `epsilon/3 + epsilon/3 + epsilon/3 = epsilon`.

## 13. How this differs from Boykin's published proof

The relevant paper material is in
[`Boykin.tex`](../../../../reference/cliff/Boykin.tex), principally the
SU(2)/SO(3) discussion and universality proof around source lines 220-420 and
the cyclotomic appendix around lines 1080-1125.

### What is shared with Boykin

The formalization uses the following core Boykin ideas:

| Boykin idea | Lean realization |
|---|---|
| `exp(i phi n.sigma) = cos(phi)I + i sin(phi)n.sigma` | `axisRotation_closed_form` |
| Concrete `A = sigma_z^(-1/4) sigma_x^(1/4)` | `boykinA` and `boykinA_is_axisRotation` |
| Concrete conjugate rotation `B` | `boykinB` and `boykinB_is_axisRotation` |
| The two rotation axes are orthogonal | `boykin_axes_orthogonal` |
| `cos(lambda*pi) = (1/2)(1+1/sqrt(2))` | `boykinLambda` and `boykinLambda_cos_axis` |
| Boykin's quartic polynomial for `zeta` | `boykinPolynomial` and `boykinZeta_satisfies_polynomial` |
| Irrationality gives dense powers | `axisRotation_powers_dense` and the A/B density theorems |
| Three rotations about two orthogonal axes suffice | `boykin_euler_product_expansion` and `standardZ_axisRotation_boykin_euler` |

### What Lean proves differently

#### 1. The final theorem is specialized to `Rz`, not all of `SU(2)`

Boykin states density in all of `SU(2)` and invokes a general Euler
decomposition.

The transitive dependency closure of `HT_Rz_dense` contains no theorem saying
that every `SU(2)` matrix has Boykin-axis Euler angles. Instead,
`standardZ_axisRotation_boykin_euler` constructs angles only for the standard
`z`-axis rotation required by Lemma 12.

This is sufficient for the Clifford+T universality chain because Lemma 12 asks
only for approximation of `Rz(theta)`.

#### 2. The proof avoids the SO(3)-to-SU(2) local-isomorphism argument

Boykin motivates the Euler formula through the local isomorphism between
`SO(3)` and `SU(2)`.

Lean works directly with complex `2 x 2` matrices in the
`su2Pair(a,u) = aI + i u.sigma` representation. It proves the multiplication
law and the Euler product identity directly. No formal local-isomorphism
theorem is used.

#### 3. Euler inversion is explicit rather than asserted

Boykin says that the displayed scalar/vector equations can be inverted.

Lean performs one explicit inversion for the target `z` axis using:

- an orthonormal basis built from `n1,n2,n1 cross n2`;
- coordinates `c1,c2,c3`;
- three applications of complex polar coordinates through `Complex.arg`.

#### 4. Irrationality does not formalize the full cyclotomic theorem

Boykin says the quartic is an irreducible monic non-cyclotomic polynomial and
uses the appendix's minimal-polynomial/cyclotomic equivalence.

Lean proves only the implication actually needed:

```text
lambda rational -> zeta is a root of unity.
```

It then derives a contradiction from algebraic integrality:

```text
zeta root of unity
  -> zeta + zeta^(-1) integral
  -> 7/4 integral
  -> contradiction.
```

Thus the Lean proof uses Boykin's concrete polynomial equation but not the
paper's irreducibility claim and not a generic Kronecker theorem.

#### 5. Lean uses integer powers

The paper informally writes positive powers when discussing density.

Mathlib's dense additive subgroup theorem naturally gives integer
coefficients. Lean therefore obtains `k : Z`. Negative powers are then
implemented by ordinary finite `{H,T}` words using `H^(-1)=H` and
`T^(-1)=T^7`.

This does not enlarge the physical gate set.

#### 6. Lean makes topology and error propagation explicit

Boykin writes phase approximation with an informal `approximately` relation.

Lean explicitly proves:

- density of the additive subgroup generated by `lambda*pi` and `2*pi`;
- continuity of the matrix rotation map;
- continuity of Hilbert-Schmidt distance along that map;
- a three-factor Hilbert-Schmidt product bound;
- exact allocation of the error budget into three pieces.

#### 7. Lean proves that all abstract powers are actual circuits

The paper treats the real-power identities and finite gate generation at a
higher level.

Lean defines every required word, proves its evaluation, constructs inverse
words, and proves evaluation of arbitrary integer powers.

## Final logical summary

The proof of Lemma 12 is not merely "Boykin proves `{H,T}` is dense in
`SU(2)`." Its actual formal logic is narrower and more explicit:

```text
Boykin concrete matrices A and B
  + exact proofs that they are HT circuits
  + exact proofs that they rotate about orthogonal axes by lambda*pi
  + an elementary proof that lambda is irrational
  + Mathlib density of an irrational additive subgroup
  + continuity of axis rotations and hsDistance
  + explicit Euler inversion for the standard z-axis only
  + a proved three-factor Hilbert-Schmidt error inequality
  => every rz(theta) is approximable by an HT circuit.
```

That is the precise proof tree leading to `HT_Rz_dense` and hence to the
public Lemma 12 theorem used by the Clifford+T universality proof.
